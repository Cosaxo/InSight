# The serving policy is the experiment

*Paper 0a of the axiom series: population-scale sequential design when each person yields one observation a day.*

**Status: theory research.** Nothing here describes the app or proposes a change to it; `research/README.md` says what a paper is and how one reaches the product. This paper develops the first layer of paper 0's pattern theory, section 4 there.

**Perfect-form test.** If the app did not exist, this would still be worth saying because every measurement system that asks people questions spends a fixed budget of attention, and the theory of how to spend that budget to learn the most about a population and about each person in it, across many axes at once, has not been written for a system of this shape.

**Abstract.** Paper 0 claimed that when human attention is the binding resource, the most efficient pattern-finder is the choice of what to observe. This paper develops that claim. It treats the daily choice of which item to serve each person as one sequential experiment run on the whole population, states its objective as an explicit scalarization in a single currency, bits of predictive information about the population's couplings, each person's placement and each person's dynamics, per unit of attention, and argues where the choice of observation dominates any estimator and where it does not: design is the whole answer for a population coupling on axes never jointly observed, and partial pooling is most of the answer for a person, whose own daily observations carry a few hundred bits a year. It then sets out the constraints that keep the objective honest: a spine of shared items so that every person is placed in one geometry and every pair of axes is jointly observed on a core; repetition schedules for state items and tests, set by the timescale of the change to be seen and bounded by what repetition teaches; deliberate allocation of overlap where the pairing ledger shows two axes with too few people in common; a survival term, because a person who stops answering yields nothing forever; a coverage term, because an information-seeking policy left to itself starves the cohorts it finds least surprising; a probability floor under every eligible item and a uniform stream never selected on, because a deterministic choice destroys the overlap that makes selection estimable; and a record of every serving decision with its realised probability, so that selection is a known function of recorded state rather than a hidden bias. It describes the loop by which the policy learns the realised value of every item and admits or retires items on that value, and the event-triggered form in which the policy asks what is most informative right after something has happened. It states what becomes knowable because of the design itself, the conditions under which the theory holds, the requirements scale generates, and the open problems, foremost the tractability of non-myopic design at population scale and the weighting of person-level against population-level targets, which is a research choice and not a theorem.

**1 · Found data and chosen data**

Most theory of finding patterns assumes the data are given. A dataset exists, and the question is what to compute from it. The system this series is about does not have that shape. Its data are chosen, one item at a time, for one person at a time, every day, from a bank with no ceiling. Nothing exists in the tensor of paper 0 until the policy decides to put it there, and the policy can fill only what a person's daily attention budget allows, one cell a day when the cells are single items, less when the observation is a keyed test that costs an hour. A different policy produces a different dataset, and the two datasets can differ by orders of magnitude in what can be learned from them, with identical people answering honestly in both.

That is the reversal this paper is about. When the data are found, the estimator is the whole of the method. When the data are chosen under a hard budget, the estimator is downstream of the choice, and the choice carries most of the value. For the population's couplings, a reasonable estimator on a well-chosen dataset beats an optimal estimator on a badly chosen one, and under a daily attention budget the gap is not a refinement. Section 3 says where that holds and where it reverses.

**2 · The decision, and its currency**

Each day, for each person, the policy chooses one observation: an item from the bank, a sitting of a keyed test, a turn in a guessing game, a report on the body, or nothing. The objective is stated in the currency of paper 0's section 7: the expected number of bits the observation will save in predicting everything the system will later observe. Three targets share that currency and the policy serves all three.

The first target is the population's couplings: the cross-loadings, couplings and relationship terms of paper 0's three geometries. An observation is worth more to this target when it falls on a coupling that is poorly identified, and worth almost nothing when the coupling is already pinned. The second target is the person's placement: their position in the between-person geometry. An observation is worth more when it falls at the person's frontier, an item whose answer the model cannot yet predict for them, and worth nothing when the answer is already predictable. The third target is the person's dynamics: their within-person state space and its timescales. An observation is worth more when it repeats a state item at a lag that resolves a timescale not yet resolved.

Three targets in one currency are comparable only once three things are stated. The utility: log-loss is what makes bits the unit, and a target for which the natural criterion is variance reduction, such as the precision of one coupling coefficient, orders items differently, so the objective names which it is optimizing. The horizon: an observation whose value is that it enables a later crossing has a value in the future term, and a policy that drops the future term is the myopic special case. And the weight: how many bits about the population are worth one bit about this person. There is no theorem that sets the weight. It is a research choice, made explicit and revisited, and stating it is what makes the objective a design rather than a slogan. One more term sits outside the sum. Expected information gain is defined inside the current model and cannot value an observation whose worth is that it would show the model wrong, so the objective carries an exploration allocation outside the current model class, and that allocation is what funds the self-extension paper 0 describes.

**3 · Where design dominates estimation, and where it does not**

Consider one person and one day. Among all items the bank could serve, the information an answer carries about that person ranges from nothing, for an item whose answer the model already predicts with near certainty, to the full entropy of the item, for an item at the person's frontier. For a binary item that range runs from roughly nothing to one bit; for a multi-option item, more. The estimator cannot recover what the observation did not carry. So the ratio between a good and a bad serving decision, for that person on that day, is the ratio between the frontier item's entropy and the predictable item's, which is unbounded.

Across a population the same holds for the couplings. An observation that jointly observes two axes on a person who holds both, where few such people have been observed, moves a coupling's identification interval; an observation that lands on a coupling already estimated to precision moves nothing. Multiply either ratio by persons and by days, and the total information the system gathers under a good policy against a careless one differs by a factor no estimator closes.

This is the sense in which the policy is the pattern-finder for the population. Every coupling the estimation layer will later find is a coupling the policy chose to make observable, and a pair of axes never jointly observed is one no estimator recovers.

At the level of one person the regime reverses. A person's own observations under a daily attention budget carry a few hundred bits a year, so most of what the system can say about any one person is borrowed from the population through partial pooling, and the estimator's choice of how to pool is worth more than the next item served. The two regimes are separated by how much of the target's information lives in the unit versus in the population. A policy that forgot the second regime would over-ask its people for readings the prior already supplies; an estimator that forgot the first would search for couplings in observations that carry none.

**4 · The constraints that make the objective honest**

An objective of pure expected information, applied naively, produces a dataset that is maximally informative and useless. Each constraint below removes one way it fails.

*4.1 The spine.* Comparison needs shared ground. A set of items is served to everyone, on a schedule, regardless of expected gain: the anchor set that places every person in one geometry and observes every pair of axes jointly on a core. Without it the objective drifts each person toward their own idiosyncratic frontier, and the population becomes a collection of people measured on disjoint items who cannot be compared. The spine is also the floor of the pairing ledger: it guarantees that no coupling is ever entirely unidentified, only imprecise.

*4.2 Repetition schedules.* Within-person readings need repeats, and repeats are governed by two opposing forces. To see a change with a given timescale, an item must be re-served at least twice per period of that change, and to see which timescale a trait moves on, several lags are needed. Against that, a repeated item teaches itself: familiarity and memory of the last answer contaminate the repeat. So the schedule for each state item is designed from the timescale hypothesis for the trait it measures and bounded by that item's measured learning curve, and where a keyed test is repeated, first exposure is staggered across people so that practice is decorrelated from age and from calendar time by design. A gain that is measured and subtracted takes real signal with it; a gain that is decorrelated by design does not.

*4.3 Pairing allocation.* A coupling between two axes is identified only by people who supplied both. The pairing ledger of paper 0 names, for every pair of axes, how many people that is and how wide the coupling's interval remains. Where the ledger shows a gap, the policy steers: people who hold an expensive axis, a genome or a body stream, are served the items of the axes it most needs to be crossed with, and people in a guessing game are served the items their partners were asked to guess. Steering depends only on pre-response variables, which axes a person holds and the counts in the ledger, never on the current fit; where it must depend on the fit, the coupling is reported with the steering propensity, because steering that conditions on a posterior conditions on a downstream variable. This is planned missingness. The population as a whole is sampled in a matrix, each person seeing a designed subset, so that population estimands are identified across people without any one person answering everything; and the expensive axes are paired by deliberate allocation rather than by hoping the overlap occurs.

*4.4 The survival term.* A person who stops answering yields zero bits forever, so expected future information is conditional on continued participation, and the objective carries a survival term: the expected gain of an item is weighted by the probability the person is still answering tomorrow, and that probability depends on what they were served today. This is how interest enters the design without becoming its goal. An item that is dull to a person but informative is served when the survival cost is small and withheld when it is not; an item that is delightful and uninformative is never the objective's choice, though it may be the survival term's. The two are traded in one currency, and the trade is visible.

*4.5 The coverage term.* An information-seeking policy left to itself concentrates attention where the model is most uncertain, which over time is where the model has learned least, which is disproportionately the cohorts that are rare, that answer differently, or that were served badly early. Without a coverage term the policy starves exactly the parts of the population whose couplings differ from the majority's, and the estimation layer then reports the majority's structure as everyone's. So the objective carries a term that values information about a cohort in proportion to how little the model can say about it, so that a rare cohort's frontier is worth more per observation than a common one's. Fairness of measurement is a design property here, not a review.

*4.6 The policy is stochastic, and records itself.* A deterministic choice of the best item assigns every other eligible item a serving probability of zero, and with that it destroys the overlap that makes selection estimable: there is no counterfactual arm against which the selection term of paper 0's section 5.4 can be estimated, no residual on items never served, and no reading of what a person would have answered. So every eligible item carries a serving probability bounded below by a floor, a fixed fraction of each person's days is drawn uniformly from the spine and never selected on, and every served item carries the reason it was served and the probability with which it was served: which target it was chosen for, what the expected gain was, what the survival and coverage terms contributed, what the person's state was, and the realised propensity. Selection is then a known function of recorded variables, and the honesty terms of paper 0's section 5.4 are estimable rather than assumed, because the missingness of every unserved item is missing by a recorded design and not by an unknown process. A policy that does not record itself produces a dataset whose selection cannot be modelled, and every pattern in it is a pattern in the serving. The uniform fraction is also the evaluation surface: a model's code length is read on that stream, because on the adaptive stream the policy can improve the number by serving predictable items, and the two streams are never averaged.

**5 · Identification by design**

Sections 4.1 and 4.3 together are an identification argument, and it is worth stating on its own. A cross-axis coupling is a population quantity that only joint observation identifies; no size of separately observed samples fixes it. So the question of what the system can ever know about a coupling is answered before any estimation, by the design: has this pair of axes been jointly observed, on whom, and how often. The spine guarantees a floor for every pair. Pairing allocation raises the floor where the couplings matter most. The ledger reports the result. And the estimation layer inherits an identified problem rather than discovering, after the fact, that the structure it wants is an interval.

The same argument bounds the value of an expensive axis. A genome on a person who has answered nothing pairs with nothing. Its value to the system is realised only as the policy serves that person the items its crossings need, so the decision to admit an expensive axis is also a decision to allocate attention to the people who hold it, and the two are priced together.

**6 · Time, and events, as design variables**

The lag between repeats is one time variable; when to ask is another. Some observations are worth most immediately after something has happened. After a reveal in a guessing game, the items that measure how a person updates on being read or misread are at their most informative. After a reported sick day, the state items that separate the body's effect from the day's noise are. After a change in a tie, the items about the relationship are. So the policy carries an event-triggered form: an event with a timestamp raises the expected gain of the items whose information depends on proximity to it, and the causal layer of paper 0's section 6 feeds the design directly. The order of measurement is fixed as part of the trigger, a state report before a test rather than after, or the direction of what is then read is not identifiable.

Over a longer horizon the policy schedules the axes themselves. A keyed test is re-served on a schedule of years, staggered as 4.2 requires. A within-person series is planned to reach the number of occasions its timescale needs before the series is read. The system's continuity is part of its design, and a policy that plans years ahead is measuring things no one-shot design can.

**7 · The loop: the policy learns the value of every item**

An item enters the bank with a prior on its information, inherited from the family of items it resembles, and never with known parameters. Each time it is served, its realised information, the bits it actually saved in predicting what came after, updates that prior, per cohort and per kind of person. Over time every item carries a measured value, and the bank is pruned and grown on that value: an item whose realised information stays below what the items already in the bank predict about the same thing is redundant and retired, whatever its face appeal, and a new item is admitted only where the model's residuals, paper 0's section 7, say that structure exists which no current item reaches.

This loop is the same shape as the policy's own choice, one level up. The policy chooses among items by expected gain; the bank chooses among candidate items by realised gain; and both run in the one currency, so the admission bar for a new item and the serving decision for an old one are comparable quantities. A bank of unbounded size is manageable only because of this: its size is not a cost, because the policy serves by value and the bank retires by value, and an item that is never worth serving costs nothing but a row.

**8 · What becomes knowable because of the design**

The design is not only a means to the readings of the crossing papers. It produces readings of its own, which nothing found can produce.

The information map of the bank: for every item and every cohort, how much an answer to it separates people, and therefore where the population's variation actually lies, which is a fact about the population that only a designed sweep across an unbounded bank reveals.

The cost of every coupling in attention: how many observations it took, on how many people, to pin each cross-axis coupling to a given precision. That number is the price list of the science, and it is what lets the value of adding an axis be compared with the value of asking more of an existing one.

The timescales of change, per trait: the repetition schedules of 4.2 are hypotheses about how fast each trait moves, and their results are the first measurement of those timescales on a population, which is the within-person frontier paper 0's section 5.2 names.

And the value of each axis in bits, measured rather than argued, on the uniform stream: the currency of paper 0 becomes a number for each axis only because the policy recorded what each axis's observations saved on observations it did not choose. The compounding cycle, a prior on one axis lowering the cost of another, is read directly from that record.

**9 · What would have to hold**

The expected gains are computable. A policy that chooses by expected information needs a model good enough to compute it, and a model that is wrong chooses badly in a way that confirms itself. So the policy carries an exploration term that serves some items regardless of expected gain, and the realised-information loop of section 7 is what corrects the model's estimate of value.

The survival model is honest. A survival term estimated from the people who stayed is biased toward what kept them; the policy's own record, 4.6, is what lets the leaving hazard be estimated on everyone who was served, including those who left, and the response process and the hazard are estimated jointly, because survival in an attention-priced population is correlated with the traits being measured.

The spine is answered. A spine that a fraction of the population skips is a floor with holes, and the ledger reports the holes rather than the design.

The computation is bounded. The per-person, per-day decision runs over an unbounded bank, so it runs on aggregates: the expected gain of an item for a person is computed from the item's measured value for people like them and the person's current posterior, not from a search over the whole bank every day.

The policy may see only what custody allows. Where an axis is device-held, the part of the decision that depends on it runs on the device, in the same shape as paper 0's section 3.3, and the recorded reason of 4.6 travels without the raw.

**10 · The requirements scale generates**

A frontier for every person needs a bank deep enough that every person has one, which is the requirement behind an unbounded bank. Pairing allocation needs enough holders of every expensive axis for the ledger's floor to rise, which is a requirement on population and on consent built, not on ambition. The coverage term needs rare cohorts present at all, which is a requirement on reach. Timescale readings need years, and the same people across them. None is a reason to want less; each is what the fully populated design looks like from the inside.

**11 · The potential, and what remains open**

Fully populated, the policy is an experiment on a population that never ends, in which every day's questions are the ones that teach the most, in which what is asked after something happens is what that event makes knowable, in which the bank measures its own items and retires the ones that carry nothing, and in which the price of every coupling and the value of every axis are read from the record rather than argued. The crossing papers describe what would be knowable. This paper describes the instrument that makes it cheap.

What remains open is research. Non-myopic design at population scale is intractable exactly, and every approximation trades the future term for tractability in a way that has to be measured against the objective it approximates. The weight between person-level and population-level bits has no theorem behind it and needs a principled way to be set and revisited. The coverage term and the survival term can pull against each other, where a rare cohort is also the one most likely to leave, and the resolution is a design choice with consequences for whose structure the system learns. And the loop of section 7 can overfit: a bank that grows only where residuals cluster will chase noise unless the admission bar carries the same out-of-sample discipline as the models, which is the same open problem paper 0 names for residual-proposed axes, one level down.
