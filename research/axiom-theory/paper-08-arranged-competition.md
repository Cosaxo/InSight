# What a person does when it counts

*Paper 8 of the axiom series: competitions the system arranges as the one axis where the situation is built rather than observed, crossed with the logic test, the bank and the genome, and read as an adaptive instrument whose items are other people.*

**Status: theory research.** Nothing here describes the app or proposes a change to it; `research/README.md` says what a paper is and how one reaches the product. Paper 0a supplies the design argument this axis takes furthest; paper 1 the measured ability it must be separated from; paper 2 the observers it gives a criterion to; papers 6 and 7 the worn instrument and the verification that staked play requires.

**Perfect-form test.** If the app did not exist, this would still be worth saying because every large measurement of persons is built from what people say about themselves or about each other, the oldest and least closed gap in the field is between what a person reports and what they do when something is at stake, and an arranged competition is the only instrument that can put the same people into a designed situation repeatedly, in many domains, with the stakes set by the experimenter.

**Abstract.** Every other axis in this series is supplied by the person and observed as it arrives: an answer, a file, a rating, a physiological trace. An arranged competition is different in kind, because the system builds the situation — the task, the rules, the opponent, the stakes and the timing are all chosen, and choosing them is the measurement. Three structural facts follow and they organize the paper. The opponent is the item, so matchmaking and adaptive testing are the same algorithm under two names, and difficulty is no longer a property of the instrument but a quantity the design sets per encounter. The stakes are a manipulable variable, so the derivative of a person's performance with respect to what is at stake becomes measurable, and that derivative — how much better someone gets when it matters — is a behavioural parameter no self-report reaches and no observational design identifies. And an outcome is relational rather than personal, so the model is a comparison model over a latent quantity, and every person parameter is recovered from encounters rather than from scores. From those three the paper states what becomes knowable: effort elasticity and its stability across domains; the distance between reported and enacted disposition, which is the validity criterion the question bank cannot supply for itself; whether competitive performance has one general factor or several; what a person does after losing, which is a behavioural reading of persistence and risk-taking against the reported version; the causal effect of social comparison, identified because the opponent can be randomized; the physiological signature of a known outcome, since a scheduled competition is a timed high-arousal event the worn stream of paper 6 can read; and the learning curve separated from the ability it is normally confounded with. The conditions are unusually severe and the paper puts them first rather than last: entry is chosen by the person and correlated with expected outcome, the matcher's policy confounds every reading unless a randomization floor is imposed on it, losing predicts leaving so the surviving sample is the winners, domains are not commensurable so only within-person deviations travel, stakes create incentives to collude and to throw, repetition is training, and staked play makes duplication profitable, which makes this the axis with the least tolerance for unverified accounts. Two studies would decide whether the central quantity exists at all.

**1 · The problem, stated structurally**

Almost everything known about persons at scale is known from what they said. A questionnaire is a report, an observer rating is a report about someone else, and a passive sensor is a trace of a body rather than of a choice. Even a scored test, which measures a real performance, measures it in a situation with nothing at stake and no one on the other side.

The gap this leaves is the oldest one in the field and it has never closed: what people say they would do and what they do when it costs them something are different quantities, they correlate imperfectly, and the size of the discrepancy is itself a property of the person. No depth of questioning closes it, because the instrument and the thing it is failing to reach are the same kind of object. Asking more questions about persistence produces a better measurement of what a person believes about their persistence.

Two things would close it, and only two. Observe the person in consequential situations, which nobody can do at scale and which produces situations chosen by life rather than by the design. Or build the situations. An arranged competition is the second: a task the system sets, an opponent it chooses, stakes it fixes, at a time it picks, repeated on the same people across domains and years. It is the only axis in this series where the situation is an experimental object rather than a recorded one, and that is the whole of its value.

**2 · The axis alone, and where each side stops**

*A competition alone is a ranking, and a ranking is nearly uninformative.* A result says one person beat another under conditions that were not recorded. It carries no information about how hard the task was, how much either party cared, whether the loser had met the winner before, or what either would have done at different stakes. Leagues and ladders are built from exactly this and the ability estimate they produce is real but thin: one number, in one domain, confounded with entry, with practice and with who was available to play. The ceiling of a competition axis on its own is a leaderboard.

*The self-report axes alone have no behavioural criterion.* The bank can ask about competitiveness, persistence and risk with unlimited depth and the answers will be internally consistent, factor cleanly and correlate with each other — and nothing in that structure says whether any of it predicts what the person does. The logic test measures ability in a situation with nothing at stake, so it cannot distinguish a person who is capable from a person who is capable and shows it when it counts. Observers can be asked to predict behaviour but their predictions are scored against other reports, which is a closed circle. The ceiling of every self-supplied axis is a criterion.

*Each is the other's ceiling.* The competition supplies a criterion that is behavioural, repeated and situationally controlled; the other axes supply the interpretation without which a result is a leaderboard entry. Neither alone is worth much and the pair is worth a great deal, which is the shape this series keeps finding.

**3 · The crossing, and the three facts that organize it**

*The opponent is the item.* In a question bank, difficulty is a fixed property of an item estimated from responses. In a competition, difficulty is the opponent's ability, and the opponent is chosen. So matchmaking is item selection, and a matcher tuned to make contests close is an adaptive test tuned for maximum information at the person's current estimate — the same algorithm, arrived at independently, under a different name. Paper 0a's argument that choosing what to observe dominates the estimator therefore reaches its strongest form here, because the design does not merely choose among existing items, it constructs each one.

Two consequences. The matcher is part of the measurement model and must be recorded with every result, exactly as paper 0's serving probability is recorded with every answer. And a matcher optimized for information is not optimized for the experience of playing, so the two objectives are in tension and the tension is explicit rather than hidden — a system that only serves close contests has decided that measurement outranks everything else about the encounter.

*The stakes are a variable, and this is the axis's best property.* What is at risk in a contest can be set by the designer and varied within a person across encounters. Do that at random and performance decomposes into a part that does not depend on the stakes and a part that does, and the second — the slope of performance on stakes — is a person parameter. It cannot be obtained by asking, because a person does not have introspective access to their own effort elasticity; it cannot be obtained observationally, because in life stakes are chosen by the person and correlate with everything about them. It requires an experimenter who can set the stakes and does so at random, and an arranged competition is the only instrument in the series that can.

*An outcome is relational, so the model is a comparison model.* A result is a property of a pair, not of a person, so person parameters are recovered from a latent-comparison model over the network of encounters, and everything that follows depends on that network's structure: a person is placed only against the people they met, and against those their opponents met. This is the co-observation structure the general track describes, appearing here as a graph of encounters. A cohort that never plays another cohort is a disconnected component, and the two are on different scales no matter how many results each accumulates. So who is matched with whom is not only an information problem but an identification one, and connectivity across cohorts is a design requirement rather than a nicety.

**4 · What becomes knowable**

*4.1 Effort elasticity, as a measured parameter.* With randomized stakes, how much a person's performance rises with what is at risk is estimable per person, and it is a quantity nobody has at scale. Its distribution is a finding in itself. Its stability across domains says whether it is a disposition or a domain skill. Its relation to measured ability says whether the people who rise under stakes are the able ones or a different set — and if they are a different set, then every ability measurement taken at zero stakes has been measuring one of two things that matter and calling it the whole. That is the paper's central claim and everything else is downstream of whether the parameter has real between-person variance.

*4.2 Reported against enacted, which is the validity criterion the bank cannot supply itself.* The bank asks about persistence, competitiveness, risk appetite and how a person handles losing. The competition enacts all four. The crossing is not a correlation to be reported and forgotten: the *discrepancy* is the object. Its sign says whether a person over- or under-claims; its size is a property of the person that may be more stable than either of the quantities it is computed from; and its pattern across traits says which self-descriptions are reports and which are aspirations. This also gives the bank something it has never had — a way to ask, item by item, which of its questions predict behaviour, which lets the corpus be curated against a criterion instead of against internal consistency.

*4.3 Whether competitive performance is one thing or several.* Contests in many domains on the same people give the structure directly: a general factor if one exists, domain factors if they do, and — separately from either — a general performance-under-pressure factor that is not ability in any domain. The third is the one worth the design, because it is the factor the whole idea of "competitiveness" assumes and nobody has isolated. It requires the same people in several domains, which is a requirement the design generates and states.

*4.4 What happens after a loss.* A competition produces not just outcomes but responses to outcomes, and the responses are behaviour: who re-enters and how fast, who stops, who raises the stakes after losing, who avoids the opponent who beat them, who switches domains. Each is a behavioural reading of a trait the bank asks about directly, and each is observed rather than reported. Chasing — raising stakes after a loss — is the one to name explicitly, because it is both a genuine phenotype and a harm, and section 6 treats it as both.

*4.5 Social comparison, read causally.* Who a person is matched against changes what they learn about themselves. Randomize the opponent's ability within a band and the comparison is randomized, so its effect on the person's subsequent self-assessment, their next entry decision and their answers on the bank is identified by design rather than assumed. This is a causal reading of how comparison moves self-perception, it is available only because the matcher can be made to randomize, and it is the kind of question every observational study of comparison has had to leave open.

*4.6 A scheduled contest is a timed event the body can be read against.* A competition is high-arousal, its timing is known in advance, and its outcome is known immediately and is randomizable in expectation through the matcher. That makes it the cleanest event in the series for the worn stream of paper 6: a perturbation with a known onset, a known valence and an experimentally manipulated intensity. The physiological response to winning against losing, with opponent strength randomized, is a reading neither axis produces alone. Two cautions ride with it and both come from paper 6 — arousal moves secretion, which is that paper's ruler rather than its signal, and a contest that involves physical exertion moves it further, so the crossing is available for contests that are not physical and needs care for those that are.

*4.7 Learning separated from ability.* Repetition is training, so a longitudinal series of results confounds getting better with being good. With enough encounters the two separate: the intercept is ability and the slope is the learning rate, and the learning rate is a phenotype in its own right — arguably a more interesting one, since it is what a person's future performance depends on. This is the same trajectory-parameter argument papers 1 and 6 make for a test and for a metabolic response, made here for skill.

**5 · The return path, and the uses ranked**

*The bank.* It gains a behavioural criterion, per item, which lets the corpus be curated against something outside itself for the first time.

*The logic test.* It gains the separation between capability and capability-under-stakes, which is the distinction its own format cannot make.

*The instruments and the lenses.* Each trait scale that claims to describe behaviour gains an enacted counterpart and a discrepancy.

*Observers.* Paper 2's predictors gain a ground truth. Asking someone to predict how a person will behave in a contest, and then running the contest, scores acquaintance against behaviour rather than against another report — which is the strongest form of that paper's meta-accuracy reading and the one it could not have.

*The genome.* Effort elasticity, the learning rate and the after-loss behaviours are deep behavioural phenotypes nobody has collected on a population, which is exactly the class papers 3 and 6 argue is worth crossing with a sequence.

*The body.* A schedule of known, timed, valenced events with randomized intensity.

*Verification.* Staked play makes duplication profitable, so this axis needs paper 7's guarantees more than any other; the return runs the other way too, since a contest is a natural place to issue a liveness challenge that the person is present for anyway.

*Ranked by what they make knowable.* First, effort elasticity and the general performance-under-pressure factor, because they are quantities the field assumes and has never measured, and because everything else here is easier. Second, the reported-against-enacted discrepancy, because it turns the largest self-report corpus into a validated one. Third, the causal readings — social comparison and after-loss behaviour — which need the randomization the design already runs. The physiological crossing is fourth and conditional on paper 6's own measurements.

**6 · What would have to hold**

*Entry is chosen, and it is chosen on expected outcome.* People enter contests they expect to do well in, at times they feel able, in domains they already like. So every result is conditioned on a decision correlated with the result. This is the axis's central identification problem and the design has to attack it directly: entries are *offered* on a schedule the system sets rather than only sought, the offer is randomized, and the decision to accept is recorded as data with its own model. An entry that was only ever sought yields a sample of confident moments.

*The matcher randomizes, or nothing above is identified.* A skill-based matcher makes the opponent a function of the player's own history, which feeds every estimate back into its own input and confounds every reading of performance against opponent strength. So a probability floor sits under matching exactly as paper 0's floor sits under serving: every eligible opponent has a bounded-below chance, a designed share of matches is drawn from a wider band, the realised probability is recorded with the result, and the wider band is also what supplies 4.5's randomized comparison. Without the floor the matcher's policy and the person's ability are one quantity.

*Losing predicts leaving, so the survivors are the winners.* Dropout here is not a slow leak but the dominant process, and it is directly caused by the measurement's own outcome, which is the worst case there is. The leaving hazard is modelled jointly with performance, as paper 0a requires; the offer schedule is what re-reaches people who stopped seeking; and every longitudinal reading states what fraction of the original entrants it is computed on. A ladder read at its top is a study of persistence disguised as a study of skill.

*Domains are not commensurable and only within-person deviations travel.* There is no scale on which a result in one domain equals a result in another. What compares across domains is a person's deviation from their own expected performance under the comparison model, and even that requires each domain's encounter graph to be connected to the others through people who played both. So cross-domain readings require deliberate overlap in players, which is the same requirement the general track states for any two sources.

*Stakes create incentives, and integrity is a model rather than a hope.* Where something is at risk, throwing a contest, farming weak opponents, coordinating between accounts and colluding on outcomes all become rational, and each produces data that looks like a finding. So an integrity model sits beside the measurement model: outcomes that are improbable under the comparison model are flagged as candidates rather than accepted, coordinated pairs are detectable as structure in the encounter graph, and — the strongest defence — the randomization floor above means a player cannot choose their opponent, which removes most of the profit from farming. Any reading published from staked play states what integrity model it ran under.

*Repetition is training, and practice is confounded with everything longitudinal.* Exposure counts enter every model; a person's tenth contest is not their first even in a domain where they were already skilled; and 4.7's separation of learning from ability is only available where the encounter count is large enough to estimate both.

*Stakes have a floor and a ceiling that are not statistical.* Randomizing what is at risk means deliberately giving some people more to lose, and a design that escalates stakes with someone who chases after losses has built a harm and called it a phenotype. So the stake schedule is bounded above, the bound is a design parameter rather than a discretionary judgement, chasing is detected as it happens rather than in analysis, and consent to compete at randomized stakes is specific and separate from consent to be measured. This is a condition on what may be built, not a caveat on what is read.

*Verification is a precondition for the staked half.* A contest with something at risk between accounts that may be the same person is not a contest, and the duplication it invites is exactly the case paper 7 says is otherwise unmeasurable. So staked play runs in the verified tier, unstaked play runs everywhere, and the difference between them is itself readable — which is a use paper 7 does not name and this axis supplies.

*The arousal conditions of paper 6 apply to 4.6 unchanged*, with the addition that the contest itself moves the ruler: excitement raises secretion, so the stream's response to a contest is partly a response to sweating more, and the crossing separates them or states that it has not.

**7 · The requirements the design generates**

*Many domains, and the same people in several of them*, because the general-versus-domain structure of 4.3 and every cross-domain comparison rests on players who bridge. The number of bridging players, not the number of contests, is the binding quantity.

*A pre-committed randomized stake schedule*, with its bounds fixed in advance, because a stake level chosen in response to how a player is doing is not an instrument.

*A randomization floor on matching, with the realised probability recorded*, and a designed share of wide-band matches which serve as both the exploration term and 4.5's comparison manipulation.

*Offered entries on a system schedule*, randomized, so that the entry decision is observed under variation the person did not create.

*Encounter-graph connectivity as a monitored quantity*, across cohorts, countries and domains, because disconnected components are unplaceable relative to each other however much data they hold.

*Dropout tracked as an outcome*, with the leaving hazard estimated beside performance and every longitudinal number reported against the original entrant set.

*Verified entrants for anything staked*, and an integrity model published with the results.

*Enough encounters per person to separate a slope from an intercept*, which is what 4.7 costs and is the reason a competitive axis is a years-long instrument rather than an event.

None is a reason to want less. Each is what a fully populated competitive axis looks like from the inside.

**8 · What would decide it**

*Does effort elasticity exist as a between-person quantity?* Everything above rests on it and it may not. The study is small: one cohort, two domains, stakes randomized within person across a run of contests, and the estimand is the variance of the person-specific stakes slope after the encounter model has absorbed ability and opponent strength. If that variance is indistinguishable from zero, then stakes move everybody the same way, effort elasticity is not a trait, and 4.1 and most of 4.3 close. If it is substantial, its cross-domain correlation in the same study says immediately whether it is a disposition or a skill. Nothing about this study needs scale, a genome cohort or a worn sensor.

*Does enacted behaviour diverge from the reported version, and in whose direction?* The second study runs the bank's persistence, risk and competitiveness items on the same cohort before the contests and estimates the discrepancy per person. What is pre-specified is not whether a correlation exists — it will — but the size and sign of the individual discrepancies and their stability across domains, because a discrepancy that is not stable is measurement error and a discrepancy that is stable is a trait. This decides whether 4.2 produces a person parameter or a scatter plot.

**9 · The potential, priced against what those studies would return**

A ten is a dataset after which a field's standard design changes. Each score is conditional on the first study, since the central parameter may not exist.

*Behavioural measurement of persons.* Eight, conditional on effort elasticity having real between-person variance. A population measured repeatedly in designed situations with randomized stakes across multiple domains does not exist at any size, and the reason is that nobody has had both the situations and the people; a system that arranges contests has both. If the variance is null, four — the reported-against-enacted work survives and the rest does not.

*The validity of self-report at scale.* Seven either way, because the discrepancy structure of 4.2 is a finding whether it is large or small, and it is currently estimated from small samples with narrow criteria.

*Behaviour genetics.* Six, conditional on the phenotypes above being real and on the overlap-times-reliability arithmetic papers 6 and 7 state, because effort elasticity and learning rate are exactly the never-collected deep phenotypes that argument is about.

*Social comparison and its effects.* Six, because randomized opponents give a causal handle on a question that has been studied observationally for a very long time.

*Competitive systems as objects.* Five: the matcher-as-adaptive-test equivalence is a structural result that applies to every ranked system anyone runs, and it is available to be stated once and used everywhere.

Overall, this is the highest-variance axis in the series. Its value is concentrated in one parameter that a small study settles, and the study is cheap enough that the variance should not survive long.

**10 · Open questions the data would have to answer**

Whether effort elasticity is a trait at all, which the first study settles and on which most of the paper depends. Whether a general performance-under-pressure factor exists distinct from ability in each domain, or whether "competitiveness" dissolves into domain skill plus entry behaviour. Whether the reported-against-enacted discrepancy is stable enough per person to be a parameter rather than noise. How much of a longitudinal result series is practice, and whether the learning rate is a disposition that travels across domains. Whether an entry offered is answered by the same decision process as an entry sought, since the whole selection repair assumes it is. Whether dropout after losing can be modelled well enough that a ladder's upper reaches say anything about skill. Whether stakes can be randomized within a bound that is safe and still wide enough to move performance measurably, which is an empirical question about the bound and not only an ethical one. And whether the physiological reading of 4.6 separates the response to an outcome from the response to arousal, or whether a contest is simply too hot an event for an instrument whose ruler is sweat.
