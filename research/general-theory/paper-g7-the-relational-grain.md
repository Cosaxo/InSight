# The relational grain

*General paper G7: what a relation is as an object of measurement — three things the word names, and the difference between a pair and a graph — what a directed observation on a pair decomposes into and what identifies each term, why a symmetric observation identifies a quotient and what pins it, why the recorded graph is a design object with two selections and two populations, what a relation's own time is, what separates similarity among the related into its four causes, what the graph does to every reading made at the unit grain, where a relational observation lives and what it costs, and the two forks it puts to an owner.*

**Perfect-form test.** If the app did not exist, this would still be worth saying because every design that measures a population on many sources reaches a point where what it wants to know is not about a unit but about two of them — what one makes of another, what a pair does that neither does alone, whether the related are alike because they chose each other or because they changed each other — and the object that carries those facts is at a grain the unit-level design does not have; and because a record of relations that formed is a record of the relations that went well, so that the population a relational reading describes, the terms a relational observation confounds, the level a comparison cannot identify, and the dependence the graph puts into every unit-level number, are each questions with a design answer and no estimator answer.

**Setting.** A population of units measured by K sources under an attention budget of B units per unit per period, as in G1. Add relations: a relation is an object of its own grain with two or more members drawn from the units, a type, a membership history recorded as events, and periods of its own; a pair is a relation of two, a group a relation of more. A relational source is a source applied to a relation at a period, and its response is either directed — produced by one member, the sender, about another, the receiver, or about the relation — or symmetric, a property of the relation no member produced alone: a joint outcome, or a comparison between the members. A relational observation draws attention from the members it needs. Relations enter the record because units formed them or because the design prompted them, and the policy logs which, with its reasons and realised probabilities as G2 requires. Each member has custodians of its own, as in G2, and the members of one relation may have different ones. The object of discovery is the coupling structure at every level G3 names — across units, within units, across relations, within relations, and what units sharing a group share — and this paper's subject is the part that lives on relations. This paper is the grain itself. Its first claim is that "relation" names three things that must be kept apart — a recorded membership, an observation on a relation, and the latent a relation carries beyond its members — and that a graph is a set of the first, which is a design object and not a population fact. A directed observation on a pair decomposes into a population level, what the sender brings to every relation, what the receiver draws from every relation, what the pair carries beyond both, and error, with two reciprocities across the terms; each term has a design that identifies it and a degree or a replication it costs, and one observation per pair identifies none of them apart, which is G1's constraint restated as a table. A relational observation lives on the relation, is joined across custodians by the pairwise primitive, costs the attention of two units and a coordination they must both supply, and is replicated at least twice in each direction before the pair's own term is a number — the most expensive observation in the design per unit of what it identifies, priced here.

**1 · The problem, stated structurally**

A unit-level design observes units and reads how they differ and how they move. Some of what it wants to know is not of that form. What one unit makes of another is not a property of either. What a pair does together is not the sum of what each does alone, or is, and which is the question. Whether two units who are alike chose each other for it or became alike through each other is a question about a pair's history, and a graph of who stands in relation to whom is a structure over units that no unit's record contains.

The series has handled this by naming a grain and pricing nothing at it. G1 defines a relational source and states the one identification fact that matters — what a pair carries beyond its members separates from them only by replication within the pair — and says the design must assign relations because chosen ones select on the quantity measured. G2 stores an observation on a relation on the relation. G3 gives the relational level a between and a within and puts the exposure mapping on the graph. G5 makes a relation its own sequence. G6 says a pair is not a distance. Each of these is correct and each defers the object to a paper that did not exist. This is that paper, and its claims are the ones a design must make before its first relational observation is worth its cost: what the observation decomposes into, what identifies each part, which population the reading is about, and what the graph does to everything else.

**2 · Three things called a relation, and the difference between a pair and a graph**

*A membership, an observation, a latent.* The word names three objects. A recorded membership is a fact of the ledger: these units stand in a relation of this type from this period, recorded because they said so or because the design prompted it. An observation on a relation is a response a relational source produced on it at a period. A relational latent is what the relation carries beyond its members — the quantity a relational source measures net of what each member brings to every relation — and it exists as an estimand whether or not any observation identifies it. A design that records memberships has a graph and no relational measurement; a design that takes relational observations without replication has measurements and no relational latent; and a claim about "the relation" must say which of the three it is about.

*Directed and symmetric.* A directed observation has a sender and a receiver, and it is the sender's raw value about the receiver: it was produced on the sender's side, it is about the receiver, and both facts matter for custody in section 9. A symmetric observation is a property of the relation no member produced alone. It comes in two kinds that section 4 shows identify opposite things: a comparison, which reports which member exceeded which, and a joint outcome, which reports what the members did together.

*Pairs and groups.* A group is a relation with more than two members, and its observations belong to it. Everything below is stated for pairs and extended to groups in section 7, where the extension changes one thing: a group term shared by all of a group's observations, identified only where units cross groups.

*A relation and a graph.* The recorded relations form a graph over the units. The graph is not a relation and not a population fact: it is the set of memberships the record holds, which section 5 shows is the product of two selections, and it is the object over which section 8's exposure mapping is defined and section 8's dependence runs. Two graphs are kept apart throughout: the relation graph, over units, and G1's co-observation graph, over sources, which gains a relational layer in section 5.

**3 · What a directed observation decomposes into, and what identifies each term**

*The decomposition.* A directed response by sender i about receiver j on one relational source is a population level, plus what i brings to every relation as a sender, plus what j draws from every relation as a receiver, plus what the pair carries beyond both — the relationship term, which is directed, so that i's about j and j's about i are two quantities — plus error. Two reciprocities sit across the terms. Generalized reciprocity is the covariance, within a unit, between what it brings as a sender and what it draws as a receiver: whether those who give high are given high. Dyadic reciprocity is the covariance, within a pair, between the two directed relationship terms: whether what this pair carries in one direction it carries in the other.

*What identifies each term, as a table.* The sender variance is identified where units send to several receivers; the receiver variance where units receive from several senders; both at once where every unit is a sender in at least two relations and a receiver in at least two, and the pairing graph is connected. Generalized reciprocity is identified with those two. The relationship term separates from error only where the same directed pair is observed more than once — on repeated occasions inside a window shorter than the relationship's own timescale, in G3's sense of a window, or by several indicators of one construct in one sitting — and with one observation per directed pair the relationship variance and the error variance are identified only as a sum, whose share is an upper bound on the pair's. Dyadic reciprocity is identified only where the pair is observed in both directions, and separated from error only where it is also replicated. A design in which each unit is in one relation identifies none of the terms apart: the sender, the receiver and the pair are one number, however many pairs there are, which is G1's constraint stated as the table's last row.

*The sender's own value enters the report.* A sender who reports on a receiver on a construct the sender also holds projects: the report carries the sender's own value on the same construct. The setting identifies that term where the sender is also a unit observed on a unit-level source of the same construct, which G1's anchor set makes true of every sender, so projection is the regression of a sender's reports on the sender's own value, net of the receivers' values as their other senders and their own reports supply them. Without a unit-level source on the construct it is confounded with the sender term, and a sender variance read on such a design is partly projection.

*Two constructs a directed source can measure.* A relational source that asks a sender what the receiver is measures the sender's view. One that asks the sender what the receiver will respond measures the sender's knowledge of the receiver's own view. They are two sources with two measurement models, and they come apart exactly where the receiver's own report is biased: a sender who knows the receiver's self-view and disagrees with it is accurate on the second and dissenting on the first. A design that asks only the second has measured knowledge of a self-report and called it agreement.

*The relationship term is the only part that belongs to the pair.* Everything else in the decomposition belongs to a unit, and a coupling on relations in G3's sense is a coupling among relationship terms across pairs, or between a relationship term and its members' positions. The second is the interaction G6 called complementarity — the dependence of what a pair carries on where its two members are — and it is a surface over pairs of positions that section 5 shows is observed only where pairs were assigned.

**4 · A symmetric observation identifies a quotient**

*A comparison sees a difference.* A comparison between the members of a pair depends on their latent difference, plus an antisymmetric pair term. So anything common to both members cancels exactly: a common level, a common function of time, a common function of the encounter's conditions. Where a condition of the encounter — a stake, a setting, an audience — enters both members' latents alike, the level of its effect is not identified from comparisons at all, and a finding that the condition moves everyone alike is an invariance of the design reported as a result. What survives is every contrast: the between-unit variance of a condition's effect, and each unit's effect relative to the population's. Population-wide change over time cancels the same way, so a rate of change estimated inside a comparison network is a rate relative to a contemporaneous mean that itself moves with entry, exit and everyone else's change.

*A joint outcome sees a sum.* A joint outcome depends on the members' latent sum, plus a symmetric pair term. Sums identify the members' levels only where the pairing graph attributes them: on a component with an odd cycle, three pairs among three units solve for each unit's level; on a bipartite component — two sides that only ever pair across — the levels are identified up to a constant that raises one side and lowers the other by the same amount, and nothing in the outcomes fixes it. A difference-type source on a connected component identifies levels up to one common constant; a sum-type source up to one alternating constant on each bipartite component and fully where a component has an odd cycle. The pair term, of either kind, is identified only by replication of the pair, as section 3 requires.

*What pins the quotient.* Each kind is pinned by an observation outside its own likelihood. A unit-level source on the same latent fixes the common constant for a comparison and the alternating constant for a joint outcome, and G1's anchor set — taken on every unit, placing every unit in one space — is exactly that source; a comparison network read against the anchors has an absolute scale, and one read without them has a scale that drifts with the population. A condition applied asymmetrically to the two members of the same encounter multiplies a difference of effects by a difference of conditions rather than by a common one, and the level returns. And a graded per-member outcome recorded inside the encounter takes the reading out of the comparison likelihood altogether.

*The pairing rule is part of the measurement model.* Who was paired with whom decides what the observations can identify — the components, the cycles, the connectivity — and a rule that pairs on the current estimates of the members' latents is steering on the fit, which G1 permits, since the estimate is a function of the logged past. A rule that pairs on the relational latent — predicted compatibility, predicted outcome of the pair — is steering on what the relational observation measures, which G1 forbids for any coupling estimated on that stream, so relational couplings are estimated on pairs drawn otherwise, per section 5. Either way the rule is recorded with every relational observation as G2 records a serving decision, with its realised probability. And the informative pairing differs by target: for a member's level, a partner whose own level is well known near the member's estimate; for the pair term and its surface over positions, partners across the space the units would not have chosen, which is section 5's assignment.

**5 · The graph is a design object: two selections, two populations**

*Two selections.* Which relations the record holds is the product of two choices. Units chose to form relations, and formation depends on the relational latent and the members' own — G1's observation that chosen relations select on the very quantity the relational source measures, so that a record of volunteered relations is a record of the relations that went well. And the design chose which relations to observe, with a reason and a probability logged. The first selection is not logged, because the design did not make it; it is a fact about the units, and it is what makes the recorded graph a population of survivors rather than a sample.

*Two populations, and neither is wrong.* A relational reading is about one of two populations. The population of formed relations: how the relations that exist differ from one another and move. The population of possible pairs: how any two units would relate, which the formed set does not represent, because it was drawn from it by formation. Both are estimands. A coupling among relationship terms on the formed set is a fact about formed relations, and it is published as that; a claim about possible pairs is a different number, and the difference between the two is the formation selection, identified only by observing pairs the units did not form. Complementarity — the surface of the pair term over the members' positions — is the reading where the difference is largest, because formed pairs concentrate where the surface is high and along the diagonal of similarity, so the surface off the diagonal is unobserved on the formed set however large that set is.

*The eligible-drawn stream at this grain.* Assignment is the relational grain's version of the stream the policy never chose: pairs drawn from an eligible set — units who consented to be prompted — by a rule that did not consult the relational latent, under a floor, with the realised probability logged, observed with the replication section 3 requires. Relational couplings are estimated on it, or on the formed set with the population named. The floor is per eligible pair and the number of eligible pairs is quadratic in the population, so a floor over all pairs is unaffordable in the way G1 finds an anchor floor over all source pairs unaffordable; the eligible set is bounded per unit — a candidate set of stated size — and the floor runs over it, with pairs outside every candidate set reported as never eligible.

*The co-observation graph's relational layer.* A relational source is co-observed with a unit-level source only through units who are both members of a recorded relation and observed on that source, so its edges in G1's graph are weighted by those units, and the weight is an effective count under section 8's dependence rather than a raw one. Two relational sources are co-observed only on relations observed on both. And a relational source's coupling with a unit-level source lives on members: what a unit brings as a sender, or draws as a receiver, against the unit's position.

*Survivorship.* The formed population at a period is the relations that have not dissolved, and section 6 shows dissolution is correlated with the relational latent, so the formed set is more selected the older its relations are. A between-relation coupling at a period is therefore a moving object with an exit process inside it, and it is published with the age distribution of the relations it was read on.

*The first fork: whether the design may prompt a relation.* Assignment asks a unit to relate to someone it did not choose, which is an imposition of a kind no unit-level observation makes, and whether the design may make it is the owner's. Four shapes, each with what it identifies and what it costs. Record formed relations only: no imposition; the possible-pair population is unidentified and the complementarity surface is read on the diagonal alone. Prompt, with the offer drawn by the eligible-drawn rule and acceptance free: the offer's effect on formation is identified by design as an intention-to-treat, the relational readings on accepted offers are complier readings under section 6's two restrictions, and the offer's own effect on a unit who declined is the violation named; this shape costs the unit an offer and nothing more. Assign within a consenting pool for a bounded task — an encounter of stated length and kind: the possible-pair population is identified within the pool, complementarity is read off the diagonal, and the pool's consent is built at entry with the task stated; this costs the pool the task and the design the pool's representativeness, stated. Assign and compensate, which widens the pool and buys a selection on who accepts compensation, stated as a term. The owner rules, and whichever shape holds, the readings name the population they are about.

**6 · The relation's own time**

*Formation is an offer and an acceptance.* Where the design prompts a relation, the offer is a first-class event in G3's sense — the system's decision, randomizable — and the acceptance is the unit's. So formation is an instrument: the offer's effect on any outcome is identified by design as an intention-to-treat; the effect of the relation formed is a complier effect under two restrictions stated per relation type — that the offer moves the outcome only through the relation formed, which is doubtful because an offer declined is itself an event the unit learned something from, and is named as the violation it is; and monotonicity, that no unit forms a relation because it was not offered one. A directional claim — that one member moves the other and not the reverse — needs the offer randomized in both directions with the asymmetry as the estimand, and one-sided offers do not identify it. Where the units formed the relation themselves, formation is a second-class event: its onset is confounded with what preceded it, and what follows it is read under sequential ignorability with the estimators G3 says that assumption admits, and no better.

*Within-relation dynamics.* How one relation moves needs the same relation observed across periods — the within level of G3 at this grain — with a window per dimension, a centre estimated jointly with the dynamics, and the sampling schedule bounding which dynamics exist as estimands. The relationship term has a timescale of its own, and section 3's replication is inside that timescale or it is measuring change and calling it error. A relation observed once per period per direction supports a within-relation reading and no separation of the pair term from error within the period; the design buys both, or states which it bought.

*Dissolution is informative exit.* A relation that ends leaves the record, and leaving is correlated with what the relational source measures, so the exit is G3's dropout term at this grain: a hazard beside the response process, with the link between leaving and the relational latent identified by no data and carried as a stated assumption with a bound. Two consequences. The formed set ages into a set of relations that went well, as section 5 says. And a within-relation series is right-censored by an event that depends on the series, so a within-relation coupling read on completed series is read on the survivors and stated as such.

*The between-relation level has a time.* The population of relations turns over faster than the population of units, since relations form and dissolve while their members stay; a coupling across relations is a fact about the relations that existed at a period and moves with formation, dissolution and the members' own change, and it carries a time as every level in G3 does.

**7 · Similarity among the related, and its four causes; groups**

*Four terms.* Members of a relation resemble each other more than two units drawn at random, and the resemblance has four causes that a design must separate before it is a finding about any of them. Homophily: units formed relations with those already like them, a selection on similarity at formation. Influence: members became alike through the relation, a convergence after formation. Shared context: members were exposed to the same occasion, place or group, which moves them alike whether or not they relate. Observational selection: the record holds the pairs it holds, and section 5's two selections put similar pairs in it.

*What separates them.* Homophily is read from the members' placements before the relation formed, which a design whose anchor set places every unit before any relation has and a record that begins at formation does not; similarity at formation is then measured and not inferred. Influence is read only under a randomized offer, per section 6, since units accept offers of those they were already becoming like and convergence on accepted offers is continued selection until the exclusion restriction is argued; and a claim about who influenced whom needs both directions randomized. Shared context is read from units who share the context and stand in no relation — G3's level of what units sharing a group or occasion share — which the design observes because every unit is placed whether or not it relates. Observational selection is read from the assigned stream against the formed set. A resemblance published without the four named has been attributed to whichever cause the reader prefers.

*Groups.* A group is a relation with more than two members, and its decomposition adds a group term: what all of a group's observations share beyond what each member brings. The term is identified where units cross groups — the co-membership graph connected — and where a group is observed on more than one occasion or by more than one indicator, on the same argument as section 3's replication; a design in which every unit is in one group identifies the group term and its members' sender terms only as a sum. What units sharing a group share, at G3's shared level, is the group term's population; complementarity in a group is the dependence of the group term on the members' positions jointly, a surface of higher order than the pair's, and it is read only on assigned groups for the reason section 5 gives, with the number of possible groups making assignment coverage sparser still.

**8 · What the graph does to every reading made at the unit grain**

*Dependence.* Units in relations are not independent draws, since section 7's four causes make related units covary, and a between-unit interval computed as though they were is too tight. The correction is a design effect: the effective number of units behind a between-unit coupling is the raw number over one plus the product of the graph's mean degree and the within-relation correlation of the residuals on the sources involved, and every published between-unit interval — G1's and G3's are stated per unit — carries it, as does every edge weight in the co-observation graph. Where the graph is sparse the effect is near one and the correction is a line in the type; where it is dense it is the difference between an interval and a claim. G6's pair statistics carry the same dependence one level up, and for the same reason.

*Interference.* Every first-class event on a member reaches the members it stands in relation to, which is why G3 identifies a randomized event's direct effect only under an exposure mapping over the relation graph, assigns on clusters where the graph is dense, and names the direct and spillover parts apart. The relational grain is where that mapping is defined, and its validity is the record's completeness: an unrecorded relation is an unrecorded exposure path, so a spillover attributed to no relation is misread as a direct effect, and the bias has a sign — toward the direct effect, by the spillover the record cannot see. The share of relations the record holds is therefore a number the design estimates, by asking a sample of units to name their relations against the record, and publishes with every exposure mapping.

*Leakage.* An observation on a relation has every member as its unit of protection, per G2, so a release about one member's relations composes on the budgets of every other member in them, and a release that reaches a unit's relations reaches a neighbourhood the unit did not consent for. Under a dense graph the composition is fast: a release per unit is a release per unit times its degree, and the output-privacy budget is spent at the graph's rate, not the population's.

*The two interests in one observation, and the second fork.* A directed observation is the sender's raw value and the receiver's information, and the two members' interests in it differ: the sender's candour depends on who will read it, and the receiver's interest is in reading it. Whether the receiver may read what senders reported about them is the owner's, in four shapes with their arithmetic. Nothing is shown: the sender's candour is protected and the receiver learns nothing from a source that is about them. An aggregate over the receiver's senders is shown, with the partition arithmetic stated: a receiver with s senders who can place s minus one of them — by relation type, by period, by who was present — recovers the last, so an aggregate protects a sender only where the receiver cannot partition the set, which is a property of the receiver's knowledge and not of s, and the design states which partitions the receiver could make. Per-sender disclosure with the sender's consent, obtained when the report is made and not after, so that the consent is built into the observation and the sender knows the reader as they write. Per-sender disclosure open, which is a different source: a report the sender knows the receiver will read is a message to the receiver and measures presentation toward the receiver as much as the receiver. That cost is a measurement claim and the design can measure it — a randomized disclosure arm, a first-class event, whose contrast against the undisclosed arm is the price of the open shape in the source's own units — so the fork is put with the price measured rather than asserted. The owner rules.

**9 · Custody, computation and budget**

*Where a relational observation lives.* On the relation, per G2, keyed by its members and a period, and never on a member; a directed observation is additionally the sender's raw value, produced on the sender's side and held by the sender's custodians under its source's custody class, and its reference on the relation is a reference. A symmetric observation is produced by neither member and is held by the relation's custodians, which are both members' where the class forbids travel, or the population fit where it does not. Every join across members — a relatedness, a within-pair contrast, a relationship term — runs as G2's pairwise primitive where the members' custodians differ, and where one custodian holds every column, there.

*Where computation binds.* The relational level's variance components are pairwise quantities across units under custody, which is the binding point G3 names with a cost at population scale it does not know. Two things soften it and one does not. The between-relation components are statistics over relations that are consistently estimated on a random sample of relations, so their cost is linear in the relations sampled, as G6 finds for pairs. The eligible set is bounded per unit, so the candidate pairs a unit's custodian must ever compute against are its candidate set and not the population. What does not soften is the within-relation reading: it needs the same relation across periods, so sampling is over relations and never over a relation's observations, and a relation once sampled is held for its series.

*Budget: the most expensive observation per unit of what it identifies.* A relational observation draws attention on both sides. A directed report costs the sender's budget; a joint outcome or a comparison costs both members' budgets and a coordination the unit-level design never pays — both members available inside one window — which makes relational non-response a product of two propensities and more selected than either. Replication multiplies the cost: the relationship term needs at least two observations per direction, so four relational observations per pair per construct before the pair's own term is a number, and the sender and receiver terms need each unit in at least two relations in each role. Resolution follows the terms: the sender and receiver variances resolve at the rate of the number of units; the relationship variance at the rate of the number of replicated pairs; the reciprocity correlations at the rate of the number of pairs observed in both directions, so that a reciprocity of a tenth told from zero needs pairs in the several hundreds observed both ways — the square of the reciprocal of the correlation, times a factor near one for the usual resolution — which at four observations per pair is a line of two thousand or so attention units for one construct, before the coordination cost and the two-sided non-response. Every construct is its own line. And a relational floor over a bounded candidate set is a standing charge on every eligible unit's budget in the way G1's floor is, sized by the smallest relational contrast the programme needs to read.

*Bundles.* An observation whose worth is that it enables a later relational observation — placing a unit on the anchors before it is offered a relation, so that section 7's homophily is readable — is priced as the bundle it belongs to, per G1, or it registers at zero and the relational readings that depend on it are never affordable.

**10 · The pair in the space**

G6 says a pair's outcome is a function of the two members' coordinates and a relation-specific latent, never of distance alone. Section 3 says what that latent is — the relationship term — and what identifies it; section 5 says where its surface over positions can be read at all. The relation graph embedded in the space draws homophily as a geometry, ties among the near, and section 7 says which of four causes drew it and under what design the arrow between two of them is read. Nothing in the space adds to that: the geometry is the picture, and the grain is the estimand.

**11 · What would have to hold**

*"Relation" names one of three objects in every claim* — a recorded membership, an observation on a relation, or a relational latent — and a graph is a set of the first.

*A directed observation is decomposed into level, sender, receiver, relationship and error*, with generalized and dyadic reciprocity across them, and each term is published only where its identifying design was bought: two relations per unit in each role on a connected pairing graph for the sender and receiver terms, replication of the same directed pair inside the relationship's timescale for the relationship term, and both directions for dyadic reciprocity; one observation per pair publishes the relationship and error variances as a sum and an upper bound.

*Projection is identified against a unit-level source on the same construct*, and a directed source states which of two constructs it measures — the sender's view, or the sender's knowledge of the receiver's own.

*A comparison publishes contrasts and never a level*, a joint outcome publishes levels only on components with an odd cycle, and each is pinned by the anchor set or an asymmetric condition or a per-member outcome inside the encounter.

*The pairing rule is recorded with every relational observation*, pairs on the current fit at most, and never on the relational latent for any pair a coupling is estimated on.

*Every relational reading names its population* — formed relations or possible pairs — and a claim about possible pairs is read on the eligible-drawn stream at this grain, pairs drawn from a bounded consenting candidate set by a rule that did not consult the relational latent, under a floor with the probability logged.

*Whether the design may prompt a relation is the owner's fork*, in the four shapes of section 5, and the readings name the shape that held.

*Formation is read as an offer randomized and an acceptance chosen*, with intention-to-treat by design and complier readings under exclusion and monotonicity stated per type, both directions randomized for any directional claim, and formation the units made themselves read under sequential ignorability and no better.

*Within-relation readings are on the same relation across periods with a window and a jointly estimated centre*, and dissolution is a hazard beside the response with its link to the latent bounded and the survivors named.

*Resemblance among the related is published with its four causes separated* — homophily on pre-formation placement, influence under a randomized offer, shared context on units sharing it without a relation, observational selection against the assigned stream — and a group term is identified only where units cross groups.

*Every between-unit interval and every co-observation weight carries the graph's design effect.*

*Every exposure mapping is published with the estimated share of relations the record holds*, and the sign of the bias the missing share puts on the direct effect.

*An observation on a relation has every member as its unit of protection*, and the budget composes at the graph's rate.

*Whether a receiver may read what senders reported is the owner's fork*, in four shapes with the partition arithmetic stated and the open shape's candour cost measured by a randomized disclosure arm.

*A relational observation lives on the relation*, a directed one is the sender's raw value, joins across custodians run as the pairwise primitive, between-relation components are estimated on sampled relations and within-relation readings on relations held for their series.

*The cost of a relational construct is stated as a line* — four observations per pair, two relations per unit per role, the coordination cost, the two-sided non-response — sized by the smallest relational contrast to be read, and the anchor-before-offer observation is priced as a bundle.

**12 · The potential, at full scale**

At full scale — a population placed on the anchors before its relations form, a candidate set per unit, an eligible-drawn stream of prompted relations beside the formed ones, and replication in both directions on the relations it holds — the relational grain becomes the level at which "you and them" is an estimand rather than a display. What is knowable through it that no unit-level design offers: what each unit brings to every relation and draws from every relation, apart from what any one relation carries; whether what a pair carries runs both ways; whether those who give are given, as a population fact with its interval; the surface of complementarity over positions, on and off the diagonal the units would have chosen; whether the related are alike because they chose, changed, shared, or were recorded, as four numbers; what a comparison network is worth once the anchors give it a scale, which is every contrast it holds and no level; how relations move and end, on the survivors and stated as such; and how far every unit-level number in the design was too confident before the graph's dependence was in it. The conditions are the price, and this grain's price is the highest in the series per unit of what it identifies — two budgets per observation, replication in two directions, an assignment the units did not choose and the owner must license, and a disclosure the design must price before it makes it.

**13 · What remains open**

Whether the eligible set can be bounded per unit without the bound itself selecting the possible-pair population the stream exists to reach. Whether the complier readings on prompted relations describe anything but compliers, and what share of a population accepts a prompted relation at all under each of the four shapes. Whether the design effect of the graph can be estimated on a record whose completeness is itself estimated, or whether the two errors compound past the interval's use. Whether the relationship term's timescale is short enough that replication inside it is affordable, or whether for most relational sources the pair's own term is an upper bound forever. Whether a group's complementarity surface is readable at any scale of assignment, given that the number of possible groups outruns every candidate set. What the pairwise primitive costs at population scale for the within-relation reading, which no sampling softens and which G2, G3 and G6 leave open with this paper. And whether the candour cost of open disclosure, once measured, is a number the owner can rule on, or a number that moves with every relation type and every population and must be measured again wherever the fork is put.
