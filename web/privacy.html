InSight · privacy

Privacy policy

last updated 2026-09-02

InSight asks you one question a day, shows you how everyone else answered, and lets you play the same question against people you know. This page is the long version of what that means for your data — what is stored, who can read it, and how to get rid of all of it.

The short version: your answers are public. Anyone signed in to InSight can see what you answered, under your display name, together with the age band, gender, city, country, education and relationship status you chose to fill in. That is what the app is for — seeing how one person’s answers line up with everyone else’s is the product, not a side effect — but it means nothing you answer here is private. Answer accordingly.

This page promised the opposite until 2026-08-11, and the change is recorded here rather than quietly made. Answers used to be readable only by their author, and counts were withheld below a minimum number of respondents; both are gone, and every part of this page that described them has been rewritten. No answer was ever collected under the old promise — the app had not launched and the counters were at zero every day up to that date — so nothing given in confidence has been republished.

You do not need an account

The app works the moment you open it. It signs you in anonymously — a random identifier, no email, no name, no phone number. You can use InSight indefinitely without ever telling us who you are.

Linking a Google account is optional, and it is an upgrade: it keeps the same identifier and the same history, so your answers survive losing your phone. We receive your Google account identifier and nothing else from it.

What is stored

  • Your answers. Which option you picked, for each question, and when. Stored under your account and readable by anyone signed in to the app, beside your display name and the profile details described below. You can change which option you picked; you cannot change when you answered or the details it was recorded under, and you cannot delete a single answer — deleting your account removes all of them at once.
  • Your duel answers and guesses. These work differently, and it is a game rule rather than a privacy promise: a group or 1v1 pick is sealed — readable by nobody but you, including us — until the day after, when a server process publishes that day’s picks. Once published they read like your other answers: anyone signed in who has that circle’s id can see them, not only the people who played. It is a hand of cards played face-down, not a confidence.
  • A display name, if you set one. Shown beside every answer you give, to anyone who looks — not only to your groups. It is whatever you type and does not have to be your real name. Leave it blank and you appear as “Someone”, which hides the name, not the answers.
  • A profile photo, if you add one. Optional, off until you add it, and shown anywhere your name is shown — including to people near you, if you switch that on. Its embedded metadata is stripped on your device before it is uploaded. Anyone can report it and a moderator can remove it; you can replace or delete it at any time, and deleting your account deletes it.
  • Optional details about you, if you fill them in. Your age and its band (never your exact birthday — both are worked out on your device and the date itself never leaves it), gender, city, country, education, profession, relationship status and a height band. Every one is optional and skippable, and the app works fully without any of them.
    These are what let a question show how each kind of person answered, and they are public in the same way your answers are: a copy of them is taken at the moment you answer and shown beside that answer. Because it is a copy, changing your profile later never moves a past answer into a different group. Counts are exact and published from the very first answer — there is no minimum group size and nothing is held back, so in a small group a count of one is visibly one person’s answer.
    Your city is either chosen from a fixed list of places, or — only if you tap “use my location” and allow it — worked out from a single location reading. That reading is matched to the nearest city on your own device, using a list of cities that ships inside the app. Your coordinates are not sent to us, not stored, and not written down anywhere. What is saved is the city name, exactly as if you had picked it from the list yourself.
    Location is never requested unless you tap that button, is never read in the background or continuously, and declining changes nothing except that you pick your city by hand. We do not work out your location from your internet connection.
    We never ask for your religion, health or ethnicity — there is no field for any of them anywhere in the app, and none is used in these groupings. Two things the law does treat as special categories do exist here. Your gender, if you enter it, is as public as everything else. Political opinion is the other, and it is the one thing in this app that waits for a separate yes.
    The political compass is off until you turn it on. The app can build a six-axis political profile out of the answers you give to its political cards. It does not build one unless you say so — we ask once, near the start, and the answer is yours to change at any time in your account. While it is off, nothing political is computed about you and nothing political is stored on your profile; your answers to those cards still count in the public totals like every other answer. Turn it off later and the compass is deleted from your profile at once. What that cannot reach is a copy someone already took while it was public. Nothing can.
  • A personality type, once the feed has enough of your test answers. The app works out a Big Five type from the test cards you answer, and your answers can be grouped by it and shown that way to anyone signed in. It is read fresh every time rather than stored as a label, so it can change as you answer more — and for the same reason it applies to everything you have ever answered, including answers you gave before you had a type at all. Today the groupings drawn this way are the Big Five type and — separately, on the same screen — your verified logic score, in four broad bands, if you have taken the logic test.
  • A count of people by type, on any of the four tests. The Mirror can show how many of the people it has seen in your city, your country or the world hold each type — including each political, values and social type, not only the Big Five. This is a count of people, not a grouping of anyone’s answers, and it is computed on your device from results that are already readable to any signed-in user. It is a sample rather than a census: the card states how many people it counted and where. If you would rather not be counted in it, do not take that test — a result you have not given cannot be typed.
  • Anything you write. A “take” is a short comment on a question, and it carries your display name — to a group’s members when posted there, and to everyone when posted to the world, one per person per question. Anyone can report a take and a moderator can remove it. There is no editing; you can delete your own.
  • Who you follow. Following someone is a bookmark, not a request: it grants no access, because their answers were already readable by you. It is one-way — they are not asked and not notified — and the list is public.
  • Group membership and invite codes. Which groups you are in, and who else is in them.
  • A rough presence square, only if you switch it on. The Near screen can show who is around you right now. It is off by default. While it is on, your phone sends a ~200-metre grid square — worked out on the device, the coordinate discarded.
    No other user can ever read your square. What the people inside it can see is each other: anyone standing in the same square who also has this switched on sees your display name, your type and your answers, exactly while you see theirs. Nobody who has it switched off appears, and nobody who appears is hidden from you.
    It keeps counting for up to three hours after you close the app, and switching it off, or deleting your account, removes it immediately.
  • A notification token, on phones. Only if you allow notifications, and only used for the four notifications this app sends: your group's day has been revealed, someone invited you to a group, someone asked to join a group you are in, and a join request of yours was approved. It is stored where no other user can read it.
  • Data on your device. A cache of the questions and your own answers, so the app opens fast and works offline, plus small preferences. This stays on your device. On Android, system backup is switched off for this app so it is not copied to your Google Drive.
  • An interest profile, derived from your answers. Each night we count which topics your feed answers fall into and store the counts on your account — nothing more than that arithmetic. The app uses it for one thing: fetching more fresh questions in the topics you actually answer. Only you can read your profile — it is never public, never shown to any other user, and never used for advertising — and deleting your account deletes it. It is built from your answers alone: what you merely scrolled past or skipped stays on your device and is not collected.
  • If you buy a question or an ad. A record of the contract is kept on your account — what was asked, the place and window it was bought for, the rate and the cap — and only you can read it. What a buyer receives is the same public numbers everyone reads; there is no private cut. The current prices are published in the app and in its source. Deleting your account deletes the record with it. Submitted questions and ads are checked automatically — by our own rules and by an AI reviewer (Anthropic’s Claude) that reads only what you typed into the ask — before any payment exists. Payment itself runs on Stripe, on the web, as our payment processor: Stripe receives your payment details and we never see them; we keep only the booking, the payment’s reference ids and the refund owed if the window under-delivers, which is paid back automatically when it closes.

What we do not collect

No coordinates. The app asks your device for your location only if — and only if — you tap “use my location”, and on a phone that offers the choice it is you who decides how precise that reading is. Either way the reading is turned into a city name on your device and then discarded. No latitude or longitude is ever transmitted to us or stored, so the most precise thing we could ever hold about where you are is the name of a city — or, if you switch on the Near counter, the ~200-metre square described above, which no other user can read. If you choose the less precise option, the Near counter says it cannot place you rather than guessing a square. No location history, no background location, and no working out where you are from your IP address.

No contacts. No microphone or camera. No photo library beyond the single picture you choose, if you choose one: a profile photo is optional, off until you add it, and its embedded metadata (including any location the camera wrote into it) is stripped on your device before it is uploaded. Today the app ships no advertising identifiers and no third-party analytics SDK, and we do not sell or share personal data with advertisers. If any of that changes, this page changes first.

How the public counts work

When you answer, a server process adds one to a tally for that question — one for everybody, and one for each of the groups your profile details place you in. Those tallies are exact and published from the first answer. There is no minimum, no delay, no rounding and nothing suppressed: a question two people have answered shows two answers.

If you change an answer where editing is allowed, the tallies move with you — and the question’s public aggregate also counts the change itself, as moves from one option to another. It counts moves, not people, and carries no names: what it adds is the shape of second thoughts on that question.

We sell reports packaged from these public numbers — a buyer gets the counts, the cuts and the same who-voted list anyone signed in can already read, compiled into a document. A report’s cuts include answers grouped by vote-time cohort, by verified logic band, and by each test’s matched type and axis bands — the political, values and social results included — all computed from results that are already readable to any signed-in user. A report never contains anything a signed-in user could not read in the app themselves.

There used to be a threshold here, below which a count was withheld, and there no longer is one. It was removed on 2026-08-11 along with the rest of the model it belonged to — it cannot protect an answer that is itself readable, and it was blanking most of what the app exists to show.

The same stored answers also give us our own daily headcount. Each night a server job folds the day’s answering into one small public summary — how many people answered, how many were answering for the first time, how many came back, and how the answers split across the app’s question types. The summary is counts, computed without your identity in it: it never contains a user id, a display name or a profile detail, and no per-person usage profile is built from it. To count returning people, the job also keeps one private pair of dates per account — the first and most recent day you answered — which no user, you included, can read, and which is deleted with your account.

Beyond answers, the app sends one small usage tally a day — and it is built not to know who it came from. Once a day is over, your device may send a single summary of which parts of the app it used that day — the feed, the mirror, a lens, a reveal — as coarse brackets (“1–2 times”, “3–5 times”), under a fresh random label each day. The tally carries no account, no name and no device id, so it cannot be linked back to you — or even to your own phone across two days. The tally can include which questions were shown, answered or skipped that day — in the same coarse brackets, on the same unlinkable label, so what it produces is counts about a question, never a list of what you looked at: nobody can learn that you skipped anything. Each night the raw tallies are added into the same public daily summary and deleted after that nightly fold. Some days your device sends nothing at all: tallies are sampled. There is still no analytics SDK in the app, no advertising identifier, and no usage data shared with anyone (see “What we do not collect”).

Separately, your device keeps one small usage note per day under your own account — how many times you opened the app, roughly how long it was on screen (as a bracket, never minutes), how many sessions passed without answering, and which parts of the day you tend to use it. It exists so the app can notice, in aggregate, when people are drifting away. Three things hold it in check: it never contains a question — not one id of anything you looked at, and the rules of the database refuse the field outright; no user can read it, you included — it is measurement, not a profile screen, and what is published from it is only counts of people; and each note deletes itself 90 days after its day, so the account-linked trail is a rolling window, erased entirely — and immediately — when you delete your account.

Who can see what

  • Your answers, your profile details, your display name, your photo, your test results, the Big Five type read from them, and who you follow: anyone signed in to the app.
  • Aggregate counts: anyone signed in to the app, exactly, from the first answer.
  • Your duel picks: nobody until the next day’s reveal, then anyone signed in who has that circle’s id.
  • Your interest profile: only you.
  • Your world takes and your group takes: everyone signed in, under your display name.
  • Your presence square, your notification token, and the fact that you reported someone’s take: nobody — though while Near is on, the people standing in your square see your name, type and answers, and you see theirs. These are readable only by the server — the report deliberately not even by you, so that reporting cannot be retaliated against.
  • Your crash reports: us, through Sentry.

These are enforced by server-side access rules, not by the app's interface, and in both directions: a modified app cannot read what the rules forbid, and it cannot hide what they publish. If this page and the rules ever disagree, the rules are what actually happens — tell us and we will fix whichever one is wrong.

Third parties

Firebase (Google Cloud)

Hosting, sign-in, database and notifications. Your data is stored on Google Cloud infrastructure under Google's terms as our processor.

Sentry — crash reports

Crash reporting is on; there is no in-app switch. (If you turned it off in an older version of the app, that choice is still honoured on this device.) An error report carries your anonymous account identifier and technical details of the failure — never your email, your name, or a recording of your screen. Note that deleting your account does not remove reports already sent to Sentry; those age out on Sentry's retention schedule.

Deleting everything

The privacy panel in the app has a delete button. It removes your profile, every answer you have ever given, every take you have written, your follows — including the ones other people hold of you — your group memberships, your picks and name inside past group reveals, your presence square, your notification tokens, and your sign-in itself. It also clears the app's data on the device you ran it from. There is no undo and no grace period.

Two things intentionally survive, and neither can identify you: the anonymous aggregate counts your answers contributed to (there is no per-person attribution in them to unwind), and any error reports already sent, until they age out.

If you no longer have the app installed, the account deletion page carries the same list and a route that does not need the button.

Children

InSight is not directed at children under 13, and we do not knowingly collect data from them. There is little to collect: no account is required and no personal details are requested.

Security

Access rules are enforced on the server and covered by an automated test suite that runs on every change — including tests that deliberately attempt to read the few things the rules still forbid (a sealed duel pick, a presence square, who reported a take) and must fail. If you find something that should not be possible, please tell us: see SECURITY.md in the project repository.

Changes

If this policy changes in a way that affects what is collected or who can read it, the date at the top changes and the app will say so.

Contact

Questions, deletion requests and security disclosures — contact us at the support address listed on the terms of service page.

If you are in the EEA

InSight is operated from Norway, so the GDPR applies in full. If you are in the EEA and believe your data is being handled in a way the GDPR does not allow, you have the right to lodge a complaint with your local data protection supervisory authority. In Norway that is Datatilsynet (datatilsynet.no). You can do that whether or not you have raised it with us first.
